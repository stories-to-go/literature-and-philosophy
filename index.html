c. 1225–1274

Thomas Aquinas

Faith, reason and the common good

An Italian philosopher and theologian, Aquinas brought Aristotle’s philosophy into conversation with Christian belief. In the Summa Theologica, he explored God, human action, law and justice. His synthesis connects rational inquiry with faith and asks how social and political life can serve the common good.

354–430

Saint Augustine

The inward journey and the Christian self

Born in Roman North Africa, Augustine became one of the most influential Christian thinkers. His Confessions explores memory, desire, guilt and conversion through the story of his own life. He asks what makes a person who they are, why the will is divided, and where lasting happiness can be found.

A biblical figure

Job

Innocent suffering and the problem of evil

Job is the central figure of a biblical book about a righteous person who suffers devastating losses. His story challenges the idea that suffering always follows wrongdoing. Through protest, debate and an encounter with God, the book asks how faith and justice can be understood when the innocent suffer.

1882–1941

James Joyce

From religion to art

An Irish novelist and a major figure of modernist literature, Joyce explored how language and memory shape human experience. In A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man, Stephen Dedalus struggles with religious and national expectations while seeking freedom as an artist. Art becomes a way to examine inherited beliefs and create an independent life.

1483–1546

Martin Luther

Faith, conscience and the Reformation

A German theologian and a central figure of the Protestant Reformation, Luther challenged the sale of indulgences and the authority of the papacy. He emphasised justification by faith and the authority of Scripture. His German Bible translation helped shape the written language, while his ideas transformed religious and political life in Europe.

c. 1660–1731

Daniel Defoe

Robinson Crusoe: survival, faith and the individual

An English novelist, journalist and pamphleteer, Defoe helped shape the early English novel. Published in 1719, Robinson Crusoe examines labour, survival, religious reflection and individual initiative. Crusoe’s relationship with Friday also raises questions about power, colonialism and the limits of self-reliance.
