A biblical figure

Job

Innocent suffering and the problem of evil

Job is the central figure of a biblical book about a righteous person who suffers devastating losses. His story challenges the idea that suffering always follows wrongdoing. Through protest, debate and an encounter with God, the book asks how faith and justice can be understood when the innocent suffer.

Classical Greece · c. 427–347 BCE

Plato

Justice, knowledge and power

In the first book of the Republic, Socrates asks what makes an action just. The conversation moves from paying back what one owes to Thrasymachus’s claim that justice serves the stronger. The later books take the question from the individual soul to the city and ask who should govern.

354–430

Saint Augustine

The inward journey and the Christian self

Born in Roman North Africa, Augustine became one of the most influential Christian thinkers. His Confessions explores memory, desire, guilt and conversion through the story of his own life. He asks what makes a person who they are, why the will is divided, and where lasting happiness can be found.

c. 1225–1274

Thomas Aquinas

Faith, reason and the common good

An Italian philosopher and theologian, Aquinas brought Aristotle’s philosophy into conversation with Christian belief. In the Summa Theologica, he explored God, human action, law and justice. His synthesis connects rational inquiry with faith and asks how social and political life can serve the common good.

From antiquity to the modern world

Utopia

Imagining a different social order

Utopian thought asks how human life might be organised differently. The text follows the relationship between religious hopes, imagined societies and attempts to establish a better order on earth.

1483–1546

Martin Luther

Faith, conscience and the Reformation

A German theologian and a central figure of the Protestant Reformation, Luther challenged the sale of indulgences and the authority of the papacy. He emphasised justification by faith and the authority of Scripture. His German Bible translation helped shape the written language, while his ideas transformed religious and political life in Europe.

1547–1616

Miguel de Cervantes

Imagination, reality and the art of the novel

Cervantes follows a reader of chivalric romances who sets out to become a knight. Don Quixote and Sancho Panza encounter a world that resists their expectations, opening questions about reading, illusion and the relationship between stories and life.

1564–1616

William Shakespeare

Justice, mercy and the use of power

Shakespeare’s Measure for Measure brings law, desire and moral judgement into conflict. The play follows the enforcement of a neglected law in Vienna and examines the distance between public virtue and private conduct.

c. 1660–1731

Daniel Defoe

Robinson Crusoe: survival, faith and the individual

An English novelist, journalist and pamphleteer, Defoe helped shape the early English novel. Published in 1719, Robinson Crusoe examines labour, survival, religious reflection and individual initiative. Crusoe’s relationship with Friday also raises questions about power, colonialism and the limits of self-reliance.

1749–1832

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Feeling, individuality and the limits of belonging

Goethe’s epistolary novel follows Werther’s intense emotional life and his love for Lotte. Its account of nature, social convention and unfulfilled desire explores the tensions between individual feeling and the surrounding world.

German idealism · 1770–1831

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

Consciousness, recognition and the development of spirit

Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit follows consciousness as it revises what it takes to be true. The encounter between master and servant shows why self-consciousness depends on recognition and how work can change the person who performs it.

Nineteenth-century philosophy · 1788–1860

Arthur Schopenhauer

The world as will and representation

Schopenhauer distinguishes the world as we experience it from the restless striving he calls the will. If the satisfaction of one desire gives way to another, how can we find relief? His answers lead to art, compassion and the renunciation of desire.

1798–1857

Auguste Comte

Positive knowledge, social order and progress

Comte seeks to organise knowledge through the sciences and to establish sociology as the study of society. The text examines his three stages of intellectual development, his vision of social order and his later Religion of Humanity.

1844–1900

Friedrich Nietzsche

Belief, values and the challenge of nihilism

Nietzsche’s announcement of the death of God concerns the loss of authority of inherited beliefs and values. The text examines the consequences for morality, culture and the task of creating meaning.

1856–1939

Sigmund Freud

The unconscious, psychoanalysis and culture

Freud examines how unconscious wishes, dreams and conflicts shape human experience. His work raises questions about the interpretation of meaning, the scientific status of psychoanalysis and the demands that civilisation places on the individual.

1859–1941

Henri Bergson

Duration, intuition and the movement of life

Bergson distinguishes lived duration from time treated as a series of measurable units. His philosophy connects memory, intuition and creative evolution, challenging accounts of life based solely on mechanical explanation.

1882–1941

James Joyce

From religion to art

An Irish novelist and a major figure of modernist literature, Joyce explored how language and memory shape human experience. In A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man, Stephen Dedalus struggles with religious and national expectations while seeking freedom as an artist. Art becomes a way to examine inherited beliefs and create an independent life.

Twentieth-century philosophy · 1889–1976

Martin Heidegger

Being, time and everyday existence

In Being and Time, Heidegger approaches the question of Being through the kind of existence he calls Dasein. Our dealings with tools, our relations with others and our awareness of death help explain how a world becomes meaningful to us.

1900–2002

Hans-Georg Gadamer

Hermeneutics, language and the human sciences

Gadamer explores understanding as an encounter shaped by history, tradition and language. His philosophical hermeneutics considers how interpretation, dialogue and the experience of art contribute to truth in the human sciences.

Critical theory · from the 1920s

The Frankfurt School

Critical theory, reason and mass culture

The Frankfurt School brought philosophy into conversation with sociology, economics and psychoanalysis. In the shadow of fascism and exile, thinkers such as Max Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno asked how modern institutions and mass culture shape what people can imagine and do.

Roland Barthes

Signs, authorship and the activity of reading

Barthes examines the signs through which everyday life and literature produce meaning. The text traces his engagement with structuralism and the changing relationship between the author, the text and the reader.

1926–1984

Michel Foucault

Power, knowledge and the formation of the subject

Foucault investigates how institutions and forms of knowledge shape what societies regard as normal, true and acceptable. His studies of madness, punishment and sexuality examine the historical relations between power, knowledge and the human subject.
